Section 1

Introduction to statistics

Statistics is the science of collecting, summarising and drawing conclusions from data, while honestly measuring how uncertain those conclusions are. In Unit 1 the lifecycle had “Explore” and “Evaluate” stages. Statistics is the toolkit for both.
📋 Descriptive statistics

Summarise the data you have: averages, spread, charts.
“The 600 trial orders took 31.4 minutes on average.”

🔭 Inferential statistics

Use a sample to conclude about the whole population, with a measured risk of being wrong.
“The machine under-fills all its packets, not just these 40.”

Section 2

Descriptive statistics

Two questions describe any numeric column: where is the centre? and how spread out is it?

Measures of centre

MeasureHowStrengthWeakness
Mean x̄Σx / nUses every valuePulled by outliers and skew
MedianMiddle value after sorting (average of the two middle ones if n is even)Resistant to outliersIgnores how extreme the extremes are
ModeMost frequent valueWorks for categoriesThere may be none, or several

Measures of spread

Range = max − min
Population variance σ² = Σ(x − μ)² / N     Sample variance s² = Σ(x − x̄)² / (n − 1)
Standard deviation = √variance  (same units as the data)
IQR = Q3 − Q1  (spread of the middle 50%)
Worked example: by hand Values 2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9. Mean = 40 / 8 = 5.
Deviations −3, −1, −1, −1, 0, 0, 2, 4 → squares 9, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 4, 16 → sum 32.
Population: σ² = 32 / 8 = 4, σ = 2. Sample: s² = 32 / 7 = 4.57, s = 2.14.
Why n − 1? A sample is usually a little less spread out than its population, so dividing by a smaller number corrects the estimate upward (Bessel’s correction).

Data distribution and the normal distribution

A distribution shows how often each value occurs. Draw it as a histogram and look at its shape.

ShapeTailMean vs medianExample
Symmetricboth sides equalmean ≈ mediandelivery time (31.4 vs 30.9 min)
Right / positive skewlong tail to the rightmean > medianorder value, incomes, house prices
Left / negative skewlong tail to the leftmean < medianmarks in a very easy exam

The normal distribution

The bell-shaped normal (Gaussian) distribution is described completely by two numbers, its mean μ and standard deviation σ. It is symmetric, mean = median = mode, and the total area under it is 1 (100%). Many measurements are roughly normal, and, thanks to the Central Limit Theorem (Section 4), sample means are almost always normal.

Empirical ruleTheoryDelivery time (μ = 31.4, σ = 6.4)Real data
within μ ± 1σ68%25.0 to 37.8 min68.3%
within μ ± 2σ95%18.6 to 44.2 min96.2%
within μ ± 3σ99.7%12.2 to 50.6 min99.7%

z-scores

z = (x − μ) / σ   → how many standard deviations x is from the mean

A 45-minute delivery: z = (45 − 31.4) / 6.4 = 2.13. The area to the right of z = 2.13 is about 0.017, so the normal model predicts 1.7% of deliveries take longer than 45 minutes. In the real data it was 12 of 600 = 2.0%. Close enough to trust the model.

Population vs sample, and how to sample

MethodHow it worksExample
Simple randomEvery member has the same chanceDraw 40 packet numbers by lottery
StratifiedSplit into groups (strata), sample randomly inside each50 New + 50 Returning customers
SystematicEvery k-th member after a random startEvery 25th packet off the line
ClusterRandomly choose whole groups, measure everyone in them3 random stores, every order there
Convenience ⚠️Whoever is easiest to reach (biased)Only customers who answer on WhatsApp

Sampling error is the natural gap between a sample statistic and the population parameter. It is not a mistake; it is luck, and it gets smaller as the sample grows. Sampling bias is a real mistake: the sample systematically misses part of the population, and a bigger sample does not fix it.

The Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

Take many random samples of size n and compute each sample’s mean. For large enough n (about 30 or more) those means follow a normal distribution centred on μ, with standard deviation σ / √n, called the standard error (SE), whatever the shape of the original data.

Order value σ = ₹352, n = 30 → SE = 352 / √30 = ₹64.3. In 2,000 simulated samples the sample means had an SD of ₹61–64. This is the bridge to hypothesis testing: because sample means are normal, we can say how surprising a sample mean is.

Section 5

Hypothesis testing

⚖️ The courtroomThe accused is presumed innocent: that is H₀, “nothing special is happening”. The prosecutor (you, with data) must show evidence beyond reasonable doubt. If the evidence is strong, the verdict is guilty: reject H₀. If not, the verdict is “not guilty”, which is not the same as “proved innocent”: we fail to reject H₀.
TermMeaningRoute-app example
Null hypothesis H₀No effect, no difference (contains “=”)μold = μnew
Alternative H₁The effect you are looking forμold ≠ μnew (two-tailed) or μold > μnew (one-tailed)
Significance level αThe false-alarm risk you accept, fixed before looking0.05 (5%)
Test statisticHow far the data is from H₀, in standard-error unitst = 4.84
p-valueIf H₀ were true, the probability of a result at least this extreme0.0000016
Decisionp < α → reject H₀; p ≥ α → fail to reject H₀Reject: the app really is faster

The five steps

  1. State H₀ and H₁ in symbols and in words.
  2. Choose α (usually 0.05; 0.01 when a false alarm is expensive).
  3. Choose the test (Section 6) and compute the test statistic.
  4. Find the p-value (SciPy does this).
  5. Decide and explain in business language, with the size of the effect.

Two ways to be wrong

H₀ is really true (machine fine)H₀ is really false (machine under-fills)
Reject H₀Type I error: false alarm. Probability = αCorrect. Probability = power = 1 − β
Fail to reject H₀CorrectType II error: a miss. Probability = β
Common p-value mistakes
  • ❌ “p = 0.03 means a 3% chance H₀ is true.” ✅ It means: if H₀ were true, data this extreme would appear 3% of the time.
  • ❌ “p = 0.40, so the groups are equal.” ✅ There is simply not enough evidence of a difference.
  • ❌ Changing α after seeing p. ✅ α is fixed before the test.

⚖️ Z-test: is the atta machine under-filling?

When: testing a mean when the population SD σ is known (or n is large). The packing-machine maker certifies σ = 12 g.

z = (x̄ − μ₀) / (σ / √n)

H₀: μ = 1000 g, H₁: μ ≠ 1000 g, α = 0.05.
n = 40, x̄ = 995.38 g.
SE = 12 / √40 = 1.897
z = (995.38 − 1000) / 1.897 = −2.43
Critical values ±1.96 → |z| > 1.96. p = 0.015.

Reject H₀: the machine under-fills by about 4.6 g per packet.

📱 T-test: does the new route app save time?

When: testing means when σ is unknown, so it is estimated by the sample SD s. The t distribution has heavier tails than the normal to allow for that extra uncertainty; with large samples it becomes almost identical to z.

TypeQuestionSciPy
One-sampleIs mean delivery time 30 min? (t = 5.27, p < 0.001: no, it is longer)ttest_1samp(x, 30)
IndependentOld app vs new app (different orders)ttest_ind(a, b, equal_var=False)
PairedThe same riders before and after trainingttest_rel(before, after)
Welch t = (x̄₁ − x̄₂) / √( s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂ )

Old app: n = 296, x̄ = 32.64, s = 6.26
New app: n = 304, x̄ = 30.15, s = 6.32
SE = √(6.26²/296 + 6.32²/304) = 0.514
t = (32.64 − 30.15) / 0.514 = 4.84, p = 0.0000016

Reject H₀: the app saves about 2.5 minutes per order.

Control check: order value by app gives t = −0.49, p = 0.63 → fail to reject. The app changes speed, not what people buy. Good.

💳 Chi-square test: do new customers pay differently?

When: both variables are categorical. Compare the observed counts with the counts you would expect if the variables were independent.

Expected E = row total × column total / grand total
χ² = Σ (O − E)² / E     df = (rows − 1)(columns − 1)
Observed (expected)UPICardCash on DeliveryTotal
New113 (140.5)52 (57.4)96 (63.1)261
Returning210 (182.5)80 (74.6)49 (81.9)339

Biggest cell: (96 − 63.1)² / 63.1 = 17.2. All six cells add up to χ² = 40.85, df = 2. The critical value at α = 0.05 is 5.99, and p ≈ 1.3 × 10⁻⁹.
Reject H₀: payment mode depends on customer type. New customers use Cash on Delivery 37% of the time vs 14% for returning ones, so removing COD would hurt first orders most.

Rule of thumb: every expected count should be at least 5. For a 2 × 2 table SciPy applies Yates’ continuity correction by default.

🛵 One-way ANOVA: are the three partners equally fast?

When: one numeric variable, three or more groups. Why not three T-tests? Each test has a 5% false-alarm risk; three tests push the overall risk to about 1 − 0.95³ ≈ 14%. ANOVA does one test.

F = MSbetween / MSwithin = [SSB / (k − 1)] / [SSW / (N − k)]

SwiftShip 29.84 (n = 231) · QuickDrop 30.44 (n = 185) · ExpressGo 34.25 (n = 184)
SSB = 2,230.5, df = 2 → MSB = 1,115.3
SSW = 22,363.8, df = 597 → MSW = 37.46
F = 1,115.3 / 37.46 = 29.77, p ≈ 4.7 × 10⁻¹³

Reject H₀: at least one partner’s mean differs.

ANOVA does not say which one. The means point to ExpressGo; a post-hoc test such as Tukey’s HSD confirms which pairs differ. Control: by city, F = 1.24, p = 0.29 → no evidence that city matters.

Section 7

Python implementation: NumPy, pandas and SciPy

Each library has a job: pandas loads and groups the data, NumPy does the fast arithmetic, SciPy (scipy.stats) does the distributions and tests. This is the core of the lab file, with its real output.

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats

df = pd.read_csv("kiranamart_delivery_trial.csv")
packs = pd.read_csv("atta_pack_weights.csv")

# Z-test (sigma known = 12 g)
w = packs["weight_g"]
z = (w.mean() - 1000) / (12 / np.sqrt(len(w)))
print("Z-test:", round(z, 3), round(2 * stats.norm.sf(abs(z)), 4))

# Independent T-test (Welch)
old = df.loc[df["route_app"] == "Old", "delivery_min"]
new = df.loc[df["route_app"] == "New", "delivery_min"]
t, p = stats.ttest_ind(old, new, equal_var=False)
print("T-test:", round(t, 3), p)

# Chi-square test of independence
table = pd.crosstab(df["customer_type"], df["payment_mode"])
chi2, p, dof, expected = stats.chi2_contingency(table)
print("Chi-square:", round(chi2, 2), dof, p)

# One-way ANOVA
groups = [g["delivery_min"] for _, g in df.groupby("partner")]
F, p = stats.f_oneway(*groups)
print("ANOVA:", round(F, 2), p)

Section 8

Interpreting statistical test results

A test result is only useful when a manager can act on it. A good conclusion has five parts:

Template“A [test] showed that [groups / variables] [differ / are related] ([statistic] = …, p = …). [Effect size in real units]. So we recommend [action].”

Example: “A Welch T-test showed that deliveries with the new route app were faster than with the old app (t = 4.84, p < 0.001). The average saving was 2.5 minutes per order. We recommend rolling out the new app to all riders.”
Business questionResultDecisionPlain-English conclusion
Machine under-filling?z = −2.43, p = 0.015Reject H₀Packets average 995 g. Recalibrate the machine.
New app faster?t = 4.84, p < 0.001Reject H₀Saves 2.5 min per order. Roll it out.
App changes order value?t = −0.49, p = 0.63Fail to rejectNo evidence it changes what people buy.
Payment depends on customer type?χ² = 40.85, p < 0.001Reject H₀New customers rely on COD (37% vs 14%). Keep it for first orders.
Partners equally fast?F = 29.77, p < 0.001Reject H₀ExpressGo is about 4 min slower. Review its contract.
City affects delivery time?F = 1.24, p = 0.29Fail to rejectNo evidence; the delay is about the partner, not the city.
Statistical vs practical significance With 2 lakh orders, a change that adds just 0.2 seconds can give p = 0.001. That is statistically significant but practically meaningless. Always report the effect size (the 2.5 minutes, the 4.6 grams) and ask: is it big enough to matter?

Section 10

Summary and revision

TopicRemember this
IntroductionDescriptive = summarise; inferential = sample → population. Parameter (μ, σ) vs statistic (x̄, s). NOIR data types
CentreMean (outlier-sensitive), median (robust, use for skew), mode (categories)
SpreadVariance = mean squared deviation; SD = √variance; sample uses n − 1 (pandas default), NumPy uses n unless ddof=1
DistributionRight skew: mean > median. Normal: symmetric, 68–95–99.7, z = (x − μ)/σ
ProbabilityComplement, addition, independence (multiply), conditional P(A|B)
SamplingRandom, stratified, systematic, cluster; avoid convenience. CLT: sample means are normal, SE = σ/√n
Hypothesis testingH₀ = no effect; α before the test; reject if p < α; Type I = false alarm (α), Type II = miss (β)
TestsZ (σ known) · T (σ unknown: one-sample, independent, paired) · χ² (two categorical) · ANOVA (3+ group means)
InterpretationStatistic + p + effect size + business action. Significant ≠ important. Fail to reject ≠ proved equal

Key terms (click a card to flip)

Standard deviationclick
Typical distance of values from the mean, in the data’s own units. √variance.
z-scoreclick
(x − μ)/σ: how many SDs a value lies above or below the mean.
Standard errorclick
σ/√n: how much sample means vary from sample to sample.
Central Limit Theoremclick
Means of large samples are approximately normal, whatever the shape of the data.
p-valueclick
If H₀ were true, the chance of a result at least as extreme as the one observed.
α (alpha)click
Significance level chosen in advance; the accepted risk of a Type I error (false alarm).
Degrees of freedomclick
Number of values free to vary. χ²: (r − 1)(c − 1). ANOVA: k − 1 and N − k.

How big the difference is in real units (2.5 minutes, 4.6 g). Decides if it matters.

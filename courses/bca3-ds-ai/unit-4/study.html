Section 1

Overfitting and underfitting

A model’s job is to generalise: to work on customers it has never seen. It can fail in two opposite ways.

The bias–variance trade-offExpected error on new data = bias² + variance + irreducible noise. Simple models have high bias (they miss real patterns); complex models have high variance (they change a lot with a different training sample). Increasing complexity lowers bias but raises variance. The best model sits where the total is smallest: here, a tree of depth 3–4 with 8–15 leaves.
How to diagnose Training score high, test score much lower → overfitting. Both low → underfitting. Both high and close → good. A learning curve (score vs amount of training data) helps too: if training and validation curves are still far apart as data grows, more data will help; if they have met at a low score, you need a better model or better features.
Section 2

Cross-validation

A single train/test split gives one noisy number. We repeated the 70/30 split of SwiftTel with 50 different random seeds and retrained the same logistic regression each time:

50 random splits, logistic: accuracy 0.758-0.813 (mean 0.780, sd 0.013); AUC 0.680-0.781 (mean 0.730, sd 0.019)

So a model that scores 0.02 AUC higher on one split may not really be better: that is within the luck of which customers landed in the test set.

k-fold cross-validation

Split the training data into k equal folds. Train on k − 1 folds and validate on the remaining one; repeat k times so each fold is used once for validation. Report the mean ± standard deviation of the k scores.
Do the maths 1,050 training customers, k = 5 → 210 per fold. Each round trains on 840 and validates on 210.
Fold AUCs: 0.726, 0.715, 0.778, 0.673, 0.681
mean = 3.573 / 5 = 0.715 · standard deviation = 0.037
Every customer is used for validation exactly once, and for training k − 1 = 4 times. 
cv5 = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=1)
logit = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression(max_iter=5000))   # scaler is refit inside every fold
folds = cross_val_score(logit, X_train, y_train, cv=cv5, scoring="roc_auc")

Grid search with cross-validation

grid = {"n_estimators": [100, 300], "max_depth": [3, 5, 8, None], "min_samples_leaf": [1, 5, 20]}
search = GridSearchCV(RandomForestClassifier(random_state=0), grid, cv=cv5, scoring="roc_auc").fit(X_train, y_train)
combinations: 24  models trained: 120
best: {'max_depth': 5, 'min_samples_leaf': 20, 'n_estimators': 100}  CV AUC 0.692
Do the maths2 values × 4 values × 3 values = 24 combinations. Each is cross-validated with 5 folds → 120 forests trained. Add one more hyperparameter with 4 values and it becomes 480. Grids grow multiplicatively, which is why random search (try, say, 30 random combinations) is often smarter for big spaces.

What the search found: the best forests all have min_samples_leaf = 20 (leaves of at least 20 customers); the worst have leaf size 1 and no depth limit (0.661). Big leaves = less overfitting.

KNN validation curve (CV AUC by k): 1 → 0.567, 5 → 0.608, 15 → 0.647, 51 → 0.673, 101 → 0.674. Small k overfits badly.

The golden rule Tune with cross-validation on the training data. Never pick hyperparameters by looking at the test score; if you do, the test score is no longer an honest estimate. Other methods: random search, and Bayesian optimisation (tools like Optuna), which learn which regions of the grid are promising.
Section 4

Ensemble methods: why many models beat one

Do the maths: majority voting Five independent models, each correct 70% of the time. The majority (≥ 3) is right with probability
C(5,3)·0.7³·0.3² + C(5,4)·0.7⁴·0.3 + 0.7⁵ = 0.309 + 0.360 + 0.168 = 0.837
With 25 such models: 0.983. The crowd beats every individual. But only if their mistakes are independent: 25 copies of the same model are no better than one.

Section 5

Random forest

A random forest (Leo Breiman, 2001) grows many decision trees and lets them vote. Two kinds of randomness make the trees different from each other:

1 · Bootstrap rows (bagging = bootstrap aggregating) Each tree gets n rows drawn with replacement. The chance a particular row is never drawn is
(1 − 1/n)ⁿ → 1/e ≈ 0.368
So each tree sees about 63.2% of the distinct customers (some twice or more). The other 36.8% are its out-of-bag (OOB) rows: a free validation set. Our forest’s OOB accuracy: 0.779.
2 · Random features at every split At each split a tree may only choose among a random subset of features, typically √p for classification. With p = 9 features, √9 = 3 candidates per split. This stops every tree from splitting on tenure first, so the trees disagree in useful ways and their average is more stable.

Reading importance carefully. Tenure and support calls lead, matching every earlier unit. But data_gb (0.10) and age (0.10) rank above autopay (0.06), although Unit 2’s tests found autopay strongly related to churn and data_gb barely related. Impurity-based importance is biased towards numeric features with many distinct values: they offer more possible split points, so they win some splits by chance. Unit 5 uses permutation importance, which avoids this.

Key hyperparameters: n_estimators (more trees = more stable, never overfits by itself, just slower), max_depth and min_samples_leaf (tree size), max_features (randomness per split).

Section 6

Boosting

Boosting builds models one after another. Each new model concentrates on what the ensemble so far gets wrong.

AdaBoost (adaptive boosting) Start with equal weights on every row. Train a weak learner (a tiny tree). If its weighted error is ε = 0.30, its vote weight is
α = ½ ln((1 − ε)/ε) = ½ ln(0.7/0.3) = 0.424
Misclassified rows get their weight multiplied by eα = 1.53, correct rows by e−α = 0.65; then weights are normalised to sum to 1. The next learner is forced to focus on the hard cases. Final prediction = weighted vote, Σ α·hₜ(x).
Gradient boosting Start with a simple guess F₀ (for churn, the overall log-odds). Repeat: compute the residuals (how far each prediction is from the truth), fit a small tree to them, and add it with a small learning rate η:
F_m(x) = F_(m−1)(x) + η · tree_m(x)
Small η (0.05) with many trees learns slowly but generalises better than big steps. XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost are fast, popular implementations. 
GB staged test AUC at 1, 10, 50, 100, 150 trees: 0.673, 0.711, 0.738, 0.735, 0.728

Test AUC rises until about 50 trees, then slowly falls: adding rounds eventually fits noise. Early stopping watches a validation score and stops when it stops improving. Unlike random-forest trees, boosting rounds can overfit.

Comparing models honestly

model                    CV AUC (train)   test AUC
Logistic regression      0.715            0.747
Decision tree (depth 4)  0.658            0.698
Random forest (tuned)    0.692            0.750
Gradient boosting        0.683            0.728
AdaBoost                 0.698            0.744
random forest out-of-bag accuracy: 0.779

chosen by CV: Logistic regression
What this teaches
  • Select by cross-validation, not by the test set. Logistic regression has the best CV AUC (0.715) and is chosen. The forest’s test AUC is 0.003 higher, but picking it for that would be tuning on the test set, and 0.003 is far below the split-to-split noise (sd 0.019).
  • Ensembles are not magic. They shine when the pattern has complex interactions. SwiftTel’s churn behaves almost like a smooth weighted sum, so logistic regression is hard to beat, and it is easier to explain.
  • Ensembles clearly beat a single tree (0.744–0.750 vs 0.698): averaging fixes a tree’s instability.

Do the maths Accuracy = (TP + TN) / N = 319 / 450 = 0.709
Precision = TP / (TP + FP) = 71 / 171 = 0.415
Recall (sensitivity, TPR) = TP / (TP + FN) = 71 / 102 = 0.696
Specificity (TNR) = TN / (TN + FP) = 248 / 348 = 0.713
F1 = 2 × P × R / (P + R) = 2 × 0.415 × 0.696 / 1.111 = 0.520

Lowering the threshold from 0.5 to 0.25 lowers accuracy but raises recall from 13% to 70%. That is the answer to Unit 3’s puzzle: with only 22.7% churners, accuracy rewards saying “No”, and a 0.5 threshold is far too strict for this business.

Choosing the threshold from business costs

Do the maths Missing a churner (FN) loses about six months of revenue: 6 × ₹474 ≈ ₹2,800. A wasted retention call and voucher (FP) costs about ₹300. (To keep the maths simple we assume a call always saves a churner.)
cost = 2,800 × FN + 300 × FP
Threshold 0.50: 2,800 × 89 + 300 × 11 = ₹2,52,500 · 0.25: 2,800 × 31 + 300 × 100 = ₹1,16,800 · 0.10: 2,800 × 4 + 300 × 256 = ₹88,000 (the lowest).
For comparison: call nobody = ₹2,85,600; call everyone = ₹1,04,400. Because a miss costs over 9× a false alarm, the best threshold is low.

Section 9

The ROC curve and AUC

Every threshold gives one (FPR, TPR) pair. The ROC curve (Receiver Operating Characteristic) plots all of them:

  • TPR (recall) = TP / (TP + FN), on the y-axis
  • FPR = FP / (FP + TN) = 1 − specificity, on the x-axis
  • Threshold 1 → (0, 0): flag nobody. Threshold 0 → (1, 1): flag everyone.
  • Our points: 0.5 → (0.03, 0.13); 0.25 → (0.29, 0.70); 0.10 → (0.74, 0.96)
AUC (area under the ROC curve) = the probability that a randomly chosen churner gets a higher score than a randomly chosen stayer. 0.5 = random guessing; 1.0 = perfect ranking. Our model: 0.747. We checked by comparing all 102 × 348 churner–stayer pairs: exactly 0.747.

AUC is threshold-free, which makes it good for comparing models; you still need a threshold (Section 8) to make decisions. When positives are very rare (fraud at 0.1%), the precision–recall curve is more informative than ROC, because a tiny FPR can still mean thousands of false alarms.
